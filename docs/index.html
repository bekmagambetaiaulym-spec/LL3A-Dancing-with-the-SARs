Radar monitoring of a shared river

Qosh Tepa Canal
from Space

A 285 km canal in northern Afghanistan is taking water from the Amu Darya, a river the whole of Central Asia shares. The canal has no concrete lining, so part of that water may soak into the sand instead of reaching fields. Unlined sections across desert sands risk losing vital irrigation water through seepage before it ever reaches crops. Using all-weather L-band radar from NASA-ISRO NISAR (June–October 2026), this tool detects subsurface soil moisture anomalies along the canal corridor—turning opaque satellite backscatter into targeted inspection zones for water managers.

Calm Canal Water

A smooth water surface often reflects the radar pulse away from the satellite, so little energy returns and the surface can look dark. Wind, waves, or banks can change the echo.

Canal signature: Confirms water presence in completed sections; wind-induced ripples can shift dark specular reflection into bright Bragg scattering.

01 ENGINEERING REALITY

Engineering Reality & Unlined Seepage

A long, unlined canal through sandy ground makes seepage plausible - but a bright radar patch is not a leak measurement.

Next move

Use repeat passes to flag persistent wet-looking reaches, then prioritize those locations for field moisture checks and targeted lining. Cross-check high HH backscatter (moisture) against low HV backscatter (lack of vegetation) to separate canal seepage from legitimate crop irrigation.

285 km canal · reported unlined · field-check the signal
02 SHARED RIVER

Diplomatic Friction & Independent Verification

Afghanistan's development goals sit alongside downstream concerns over a river shared across Central Asia; disputed figures can harden positions.

Next move

Publish dates, methods, and uncertainty with the imagery. Pair satellite observations with jointly agreed river-flow records and independent site checks.

Shared evidence can support dialogue—not settle water rights
03 SCIENCE GUARDRAILS

Scientific Guardrails of Radar

Radar measures returned signal, not water volume, seepage rate, or cause. Smooth sand and calm water can both look dark.

Next move

Treat change maps as screening leads. Check rainfall, viewing geometry, and repeat observations; verify priority sites on the ground before acting.

Signal change is evidence to investigate—not proof
